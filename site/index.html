Nairobi's heat divide

Seven in ten of Nairobi's school-age children live on the hotter half of the city, where schools have the fewest trees

Land surface temperature on clear January to March mornings, 2023 to 2026, from the Landsat 8 and 9 satellites, with the people, children, schools and trees on Nairobi's hotter and cooler ground

By Stephane Ngugi, Senior Data Analyst & Researcher, Independent Researcher | LinkedIn | GitHub | Published 17 November 2026 | Last updated 20 November 2026

On clear January to March mornings, half of Nairobi County's ground was hotter than {{q1.county_median_c|c}} at the surface, and the ground in and around the Industrial Area was {{x.copy_v3.industrial_and_around.gap_c|c}} hotter than Karura Forest and the Nairobi Arboretum. This story calls the ground above that median the city's hotter half, and it is where most of Nairobi lives: about {{x.people_by_heat.hotter_half.people|int}} people, {{x.people_by_heat.hotter_half.share_pct|pct}} of the county's residents, including about {{x.children_by_heat.sides.hotter half.school_age_4_17|int}} children of school age, from 4 to 17 years old.

Most of the city's schools stand on the hotter half as well, {{x.schools.share_hotter_than_county_median_pct|pct}} of the {{x.schools.n_schools|int}} mapped for this story, and the land around them has very few trees: within 200 m of a school on the hotter half, a median of {{x.schools_trees_200m.hotter_half.median_tree_pct|pct}} of the ground is under trees, against {{x.schools_trees_200m.cooler_half.median_tree_pct|pct}} around schools on the cooler half. The satellite measures this heat at about {{q0.overpass_local|raw}}, in the middle of the school day, in the same months as the first school term.

Nairobi's heat falls unevenly

Nairobi is often described as a city with a mild climate, and on most days that still holds; yet on clear mornings in the hot season, the ground under some neighbourhoods gets far hotter than the ground under others. The roofs, roads and bare yards of the east and the Industrial Area heat up fast, while Karura Forest, Ngong Road Forest and the leafy suburbs in the west stay much cooler.

About {{q4.pop_in_hottest20|int}} people, {{q4.pop_share_in_hottest20_pct|pct}} of the county's residents, live on the hottest fifth of its land. Many of them live in dense neighbourhoods such as Kangemi, Viwandani and Kabiro, where buildings cover most of the ground and there are few trees to give shade. This story maps that exposure: where the hottest ground is, who lives on it, and how trees and buildings relate to it.

It matters now because Nairobi keeps building over its open ground, and because most days in the past few years have been warmer than normal, as the air temperature charts in the last section of the story show. It matters for the city's children in a particular way, since the first school term, from January to March, falls in the hot dry season that these maps describe, and the satellite passes over during class hours. Knowing where the hottest ground is, and who lives and learns on it, is a first step for anyone deciding where the city most needs shade, trees and cooler classrooms.

Surface temperature or air temperature? Satellites cannot measure the air; they measure how hot the surface is, which means roofs, roads, bare soil and the tops of trees. On a sunny morning, an iron sheet roof or a dusty road can be much hotter than the air a person breathes a metre or two above it. Hot surfaces warm the air above them, so the two are linked, although the air is usually cooler and changes less from one place to the next. In this story, every temperature from a satellite is a land surface temperature, and the few air temperatures, in the charts near the end, are labelled as air temperature.

Most of Nairobi's people live on its hotter ground

If Nairobi County's land is divided into five equal parts by its surface temperature on hot mornings, from the coolest fifth to the hottest fifth, its people are far from evenly spread across them. Only about {{x.people_by_heat.fifths.0.people|int}} people, {{x.people_by_heat.fifths.0.share_pct|pct}} of the county, live on the coolest fifth, which takes in the forests and much of Karen and Kitisuru; the fourth fifth, between {{x.children_by_heat.thresholds_c.p60|c}} and {{x.children_by_heat.thresholds_c.p80|c}} at the surface, holds the most, about {{x.people_by_heat.fifths.3.people|int}} people ({{x.people_by_heat.fifths.3.share_pct|pct}}).

The hottest fifth holds fewer people than the fourth, about {{x.people_by_heat.fifths.4.people|int}} ({{x.people_by_heat.fifths.4.share_pct|pct}}), because much of it is open, bare ground around Ruai and the airport where few people live. Taken together, about {{x.people_by_heat.hotter_half.people|int}} people, {{x.people_by_heat.hotter_half.share_pct|pct}} of the county, live on the hotter half of its land, and about {{x.people_by_heat.cooler_half.people|int}} on the cooler half.

The hottest wards have the fewest trees

Areas with more trees were cooler: the hot-season surface temperature fell from {{q3.tree_band_0_10_mean_c|c}} where tree cover was under 10 % to {{q3.tree_band_90_100_mean_c|c}} where it was over 90 %.

The hot side is home to most of the city's children, as it is to most adults

About {{x.children_by_heat.sides.hotter half.school_age_4_17|int}} children of school age, from 4 to 17 years old, live on the hotter half of Nairobi's ground, {{x.children_by_heat.sides.hotter half.school_age_4_17_share_pct|pct}} of the county's estimated {{x.children_by_heat.sides.county.school_age_4_17|int}}. They include about {{x.children_by_heat.sides.hotter half.primary_6_11|int}} children of primary school age (6 to 11), {{x.children_by_heat.sides.hotter half.junior_school_12_14|int}} of junior school age (12 to 14), {{x.children_by_heat.sides.hotter half.senior_school_15_17|int}} of senior school age (15 to 17) and {{x.children_by_heat.sides.hotter half.pre_primary_4_5|int}} of pre-primary age (4 to 5); applying Nairobi's 2019 school attendance rates for each age, about {{x.children_by_heat.sides.hotter half.school_going_4_17|int}} of them go to school.

Children and adults are spread across the two halves of the city in almost the same way, since {{x.children_by_heat.sides.hotter half.school_age_4_17_share_pct|pct}} of the county's school-age children and {{x.children_by_heat.context.adults_hotter_half_share_pct|pct}} of its adults live on the hotter half. What sets children apart is how their bodies cope with heat and where they spend their days, in classrooms during the hottest hours, which the rest of this section and the research below describe.

Schools on the hot side have almost no trees around them

The difference between Nairobi's hot and cool sides is clearest in the land around its schools. Within 200 m of the {{x.schools_trees_200m.fifths.0.n_schools|int}} schools on the coolest fifth of the county's land, a median of {{x.schools_trees_200m.fifths.0.median_tree_pct|pct}} of the ground is under trees; around the {{x.schools_trees_200m.fifths.4.n_schools|int}} schools on the hottest fifth, the median is {{x.schools_trees_200m.fifths.4.median_tree_pct|pct}}, and at {{x.schools_trees_200m.fifths.4.share_under_10pct_trees|pct}} of those schools less than a tenth of the land nearby is under trees.

Taken together, schools on the hotter half of the county have a median of {{x.schools_trees_200m.hotter_half.median_tree_pct|pct}} tree cover within 200 m, against {{x.schools_trees_200m.cooler_half.median_tree_pct|pct}} for schools on the cooler half. This is an association: trees are one of the reasons that ground stays cool, so cooler ground and more trees go together, and this story cannot say how much a tree beside a school would cool its classrooms.

Nairobi's ground is hottest during class hours

Landsat sees Nairobi only on the mornings it passes over, at about {{q0.overpass_local|raw}}, so this chart follows a whole day with a different record, the Copernicus hourly land surface temperature, for 27 January 2026, a Tuesday in the fourth week of the first school term with a clear morning. At {{x.copy_v3.day_of_heat.first_hour|raw}} the county's ground was {{x.copy_v3.day_of_heat.county_first_c|c}}; it warmed quickly once the sun was up, reached {{x.copy_v3.day_of_heat.county_peak_c|c}} at {{x.copy_v3.day_of_heat.county_peak_hour|raw}}, and stayed close to that until 14:00, before it cooled while clouds built up over the city in the afternoon.

Kenya's day schools hold classes from 08:00 to 15:30 on weekdays, under the Basic Education Regulations of 2015, so the hours when Nairobi's ground was hottest fell inside the school day; and the first term of 2026 ran from 5 January to 2 April, the same hot dry months that the maps in this story describe. For single neighbourhoods and schools, the Landsat maps above are the better guide, since each hourly reading covers a square about 3 km across.

What the research says about heat and children

Children cope with heat less well than adults: UNICEF notes that children heat up faster, sweat less efficiently and cool down more slowly than adults, and that they cannot concentrate in classrooms that offer no relief from the heat. In 2024, extreme weather, with heatwaves the most common cause, disrupted schooling for at least 242 million students in 85 countries (UNICEF, 2025).

Heat also slows learning over a school year. A study of 10 million secondary school students in the United States, each of whom sat the same test more than once, found that in schools without air conditioning a school year 1 °F (about 0.6 °C) hotter reduced the amount learned that year by about one per cent (Park, Goodman, Hurwitz and Smith, 2020); a second study, of achievement data from 58 countries, found that learning slowed as the number of hot school days rose, with larger losses for more vulnerable students (Park, Behrer and Goodman, 2021).

Classrooms under metal roofs are the closest evidence for Nairobi's situation. In Accra, Ghana, researchers who measured 16 classrooms for 389 days found that rooms under metal roofs reached up to 39.8 °C, up to 5.9 °C hotter than the air outside, and were overheated for 72.5 % of the hours they were in use, while rooms under concrete roofs were up to 5.8 °C cooler; adding a plywood ceiling under a metal roof halved the hours of overheating (Amankwaa, Roberts, Mensah and Gough, 2025). In Nairobi, {{x.children_by_heat.context.iron_sheet_households_pct|pct}} of households lived under iron sheet roofs at the 2019 census, from {{x.children_by_heat.context.iron_sheet_min_subcounty.1|pct}} in Embakasi to {{x.children_by_heat.context.iron_sheet_max_subcounty.1|pct}} in Dagoretti (KNBS, 2019).

This story measures the ground, so it cannot say how hot any classroom gets, and its school map has no pupil numbers. What it does show is that most of Nairobi's children live, and most of its schools stand, on ground that is hottest during class hours in the first school term, and that the land around those schools has very few trees.

What could help, and what is needed next

Trees are the cooling that the data points to most clearly. The coolest ground in Nairobi is under its forests, and schools on the coolest fifth of the land have a median of {{x.schools_trees_200m.fifths.0.median_tree_pct|pct}} tree cover within 200 m, against {{x.schools_trees_200m.fifths.4.median_tree_pct|pct}} on the hottest fifth; planting and protecting shade trees in school compounds would start where that gap is widest, in wards such as Kangemi, where the median around its schools is {{x.schools_trees_200m.by_ward.Kangemi.median_tree_pct_200m|pct}}, and Viwandani, where it is {{x.schools_trees_200m.by_ward.Viwandani.median_tree_pct_200m|pct}}.

Roofs and ceilings are the second option to test, since the Accra study found that a plywood ceiling under a metal roof halved the hours of overheating, and about half of Nairobi's households live under iron sheets.

Three further pieces of evidence would turn this map into something that schools and the county could act on: temperature sensors inside a sample of classrooms on the hotter and cooler halves of the city through a first term, to measure the air that children breathe rather than the ground; pupil numbers for each school from the Ministry of Education's register, to count how many children learn in the hottest places; and afternoon and night-time heat, which Landsat's morning pass cannot see.

      Most new building went up on the city's edges

      Between 1990 and 2020, built-up surface in Nairobi County grew from about {{q6.built_km2.1990|km2}} to about {{q6.built_km2.2020|km2}}, around {{q6.built_ratio_2020_1990|d1}} times as much.

      Between 1990 and 2020, {{x.copy_v3.growth.top4_added_share_pct|pct}} of the new built-up land was in four outer sub-counties: Kasarani in the north-east, Embakasi East in the east, Langata in the south-west and Westlands in the north-west. The older, dense areas near the centre, such as Mathare, Kamukunji and Starehe, changed little, because they were already about as built up in 1990 as they are today.

      Methods, data and credits

      How a satellite reads heat

      Everything warm gives off invisible infrared light, and the hotter it is, the more it gives off. The thermal sensors on the Landsat 8 and 9 satellites measure that light and turn it into a surface temperature. They pass over Nairobi at about {{q0.overpass_local|raw}} in the morning, Nairobi time, and together they come back every eight days.

      The thermal sensor sees the ground in squares of about 100 m. The data is delivered on a finer 30 m grid, but it cannot show anything smaller than about 100 m. A value at a single building is the temperature of the area around it.

      Method in brief

      We checked {{q0.n_scenes_searched|raw}} Landsat 8 and 9 passes over Nairobi between {{q0.first_scene|date}} and {{q0.last_scene|date}}; {{q0.n_scenes_usable|raw}} were clear enough to use. The satellites pass at about {{q0.overpass_local|raw}} in the morning, Nairobi time.

      We took every Landsat 8 and 9 scene of Nairobi since April 2013 from Microsoft Planetary Computer, removed clouds and cloud shadows with the scene's quality band, and converted the thermal band to °C. Scenes with at least 80 % of the county clear were kept. Each map is the median of the clear scenes, pixel by pixel, on a 30 m grid in UTM zone 37S. Tree cover and built-up shares come from ESA WorldCover 2021. People come from WorldPop's 2025 estimates. Ward values use the 30 m cells whose centres fall in each ward; people-weighted values weight each cell by the number of people estimated to live there. Each headline number was recomputed with a second, independent method, and both results matched. Every number in this story is listed, with the file and script that produced it, in analysis/FINDINGS.md.

      "In and around the Industrial Area" in the headline means the Industrial Area as mapped in OpenStreetMap plus {{x.copy_v3.industrial_and_around.buffer_m|raw}} m around it, where its surface temperature was {{x.copy_v3.industrial_and_around.median_c|c}}; "its forests" means Karura Forest and the Nairobi Arboretum together ({{q2.cool_areas_median_c|c}}).

      On the maps, values are smoothed between cells when you zoom in, so the thermal data reads as a continuous field. This changes how the map looks, not the numbers.

      Children, schools and trees

      The {{x.schools.n_schools|int}} schools for children on the map combine the Kenya Ministry of Education's school list, published by the World Bank, with the schools and kindergartens in OpenStreetMap, merged where their names and places match; {{x.schools.sensitivity_official_list_only.n_schools|int}} come from the official list only and may sit up to about 300 m from the school, because part of that list uses an older map datum. It is a broad map of school sites and not an official register: it has no pupil numbers, and it includes some informal schools and some that may have closed. Each school's heat is the median surface temperature of the 30 m squares within 100 m of it.

      WorldPop's maps of people by age could not be downloaded for this version, so the people in each 30 m square were split by age with the 2019 census age mix of the census sub-county around them (KNBS Volume III, single years of age, for Nairobi's 11 census sub-counties), with each ward linked to a census sub-county through the names of its census locations. Inside one census sub-county, children are therefore spread in the same way as everyone else, and differences between the hotter and cooler halves come only from differences between sub-counties. School levels follow the Competency-Based Curriculum entry ages used in the census tables: pre-primary 4 to 5, primary 6 to 11, junior school 12 to 14 and senior school 15 to 17. The number who go to school applies Nairobi's 2019 attendance rates by age group from Volume IV ({{x.children_by_heat.attendance_rates_pct.age_4_5|pct}} at ages 4 to 5, {{x.children_by_heat.attendance_rates_pct.age_6_13|pct}} at 6 to 13 and {{x.children_by_heat.attendance_rates_pct.age_14_17|pct}} at 14 to 17). All numbers of children are estimates, rounded to the nearest thousand.

      The hotter half is the county's land with a hot-season surface temperature above the county median ({{q1.county_median_c|c}}), and the cooler half the rest; the fifths split the land at its 20th, 40th, 60th and 80th percentiles ({{x.children_by_heat.thresholds_c.p20|c}}, {{x.children_by_heat.thresholds_c.p40|c}}, {{x.children_by_heat.thresholds_c.p60|c}} and {{x.children_by_heat.thresholds_c.p80|c}}). Tree cover near a school is the average share of the ground under trees, from ESA WorldCover 2021, in the 30 m squares within 200 m of it; each group of schools is described by its median.

      What the data cannot tell us

      • The air temperature people feel at street level. The satellite measures surfaces.
      • Night-time heat. Landsat passes in the morning; it cannot show how slowly places cool at night.
      • Afternoon peaks, which are usually hotter than the morning overpass.
      • Heat inside homes, including under iron sheet roofs.
      • Detail finer than about 100 m.
      • Why a place is hot. The links with trees and buildings are associations, not measured causes.
      • Who is most at risk. That also depends on age, health, work, housing and access to water and cooling.
      • Exact populations. WorldPop numbers are modelled and may undercount the densest settlements.
      • The true temperature of iron sheet roofs. The satellite product assumes ordinary emissivity, so dense wards with metal roofs may be hotter than shown.
      • Whether the city's ground is warming over time. There are too few clear scenes each year to tell.
      • How hot classrooms get. The satellite measures the ground, roofs and roads around a school, not the air inside its classrooms.
      • How many children attend each school. The school map has no pupil numbers, and the numbers of children are estimates.

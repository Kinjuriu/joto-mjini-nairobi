The same places stayed hot in the cooler season. From June to August the county's median fell to {{q1.cool_season_county_median_c|c}}, and most wards kept their place in the order from hottest to coolest.

Surface temperature or air temperature?

This story maps land surface temperature: how hot the ground, roofs, roads and tree tops are, as a satellite sees them from space. The temperature in a weather forecast is different; it is the air in the shade, about 2 m above the ground. On a sunny morning a tin roof or a tarmac road can be much hotter than the air around it. Hot surfaces warm the air, the buildings and the people near them, so surface temperature shows where heat builds up in the city.

Where there are trees, the ground is cooler

Across the county, ground that was mostly built up was {{q3.built_minus_tree_c|c}} hotter at the surface than ground mostly under trees: {{q3.mostly_built_median_c|c}} against {{q3.mostly_tree_median_c|c}}.

The chart sorts every 30 m patch of the county by its share of tree cover. Where trees covered less than a tenth of the patch, the ground averaged {{q3.tree_band_0_10_mean_c|c}}. Where they covered nine tenths or more, it averaged {{q3.tree_band_90_100_mean_c|c}}.

Trees are only part of the picture. In the dry months, open grassland ({{q3.by_class.2.median_c|c}}), farmland ({{q3.by_class.1.median_c|c}}) and bare ground ({{q3.by_class.0.median_c|c}}) were as hot as or hotter than built-up land ({{q3.by_class.3.median_c|c}}). Only ground under trees and water stayed clearly cooler. That is why Nairobi National Park, which is mostly open grassland, does not appear among the coolest places on the map.

These are associations. Places with many trees differ from other places in many ways, so the chart cannot tell us how much planting trees would cool a particular street.

Hot one day, cooler the next

The satellite maps show where heat builds up, on the mornings when the satellite passes. To see how heat changes from day to day, the chart uses the daily high air temperature for central Nairobi from ERA5, a weather model run by the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts. It is a value for an area several kilometres across, not a reading from a street thermometer, and it cannot be compared directly with the surface temperatures above.

Since January 2023, the daily high changed by {{q8.mean_abs_daily_change_tmax_c|c}} from one day to the next on average, and by 3 °C or more on {{q8.days_change_3c_or_more|int}} days. Since El Niño conditions began in May 2026, daily highs have averaged {{q8.el_nino_mean_tmax_c|c}}, which is {{q8.el_nino_minus_clim_c|c}} above the 1991 to 2020 average for the same months. These figures alone do not show that El Niño caused the warmth. This part of the story is about heat; rainfall, flooding and El Niño will follow in a later part.

How a satellite reads heat

Everything warm gives off invisible infrared light, and the hotter it is, the more it gives off. The thermal sensors on the Landsat 8 and 9 satellites measure that light and turn it into a surface temperature. They pass over Nairobi at about {{q0.overpass_local|raw}} in the morning, Nairobi time, and together they come back every eight days.

The thermal sensor sees the ground in squares of about 100 m. The data is delivered on a finer 30 m grid, but it cannot show anything smaller than about 100 m. A value at a single building is the temperature of the area around it.

Clouds block the view. We checked {{q0.n_scenes_searched|raw}} passes over Nairobi between {{q0.first_scene|date}} and {{q0.last_scene|date}}, and {{q0.n_scenes_usable|raw}} were clear enough to use. About half of the clear ones fall between January and March, which are also Nairobi's hottest months. That is why the main map uses those months. Each pixel shows the middle value of {{q0.n_hot_scenes|raw}} clear mornings from 2023 to 2026, which smooths out the weather on any single day.

Where there are trees, the satellite sees the top of the canopy. It does not measure the shade beneath it.

    What this means for cooling

    On hot mornings, the ground in Nairobi was coolest under trees and near water, and hottest in densely built neighbourhoods and on dry open land. People in the hottest fifth of wards lived on ground {{q7.lst_gap_c|c}} hotter than people in the coolest fifth, in wards with {{q7.hottest_fifth.tree_pct|pct}} tree cover against {{q7.coolest_fifth.tree_pct|pct}}.

    The data shows where heat builds up and who lives there. It does not show what any single action would achieve. Trees and shade over streets, markets and schools, and lighter or reflective roofs, are among the measures that cities study for cooling. This analysis did not measure roof materials, so it cannot say which roofs are hottest. The ward explorer can help decide where to look first.

    For more detail on Nairobi, see the WRI Cool Cities Lab, which maps heat, shade, tree cover and cooling opportunities for Nairobi and Kisumu; the IIED paper on heat in informal settlements in Nairobi, Dar es Salaam and Kariba (2026), which measured indoor and outdoor temperatures; and heat thresholds for Nairobi, Mombasa and Kisumu (Kimutai, 2022).

    Methods, data and credits

    Method in brief

    We took every Landsat 8 and 9 scene of Nairobi since April 2013 from Microsoft Planetary Computer, removed clouds and cloud shadows with the scene's quality band, and converted the thermal band to °C. Scenes with at least 80 % of the county clear were kept. Each map is the median of the clear scenes, pixel by pixel, on a 30 m grid in UTM zone 37S. Tree cover and built-up shares come from ESA WorldCover 2021. People come from WorldPop's 2025 estimates. Ward values use the 30 m cells whose centres fall in each ward; people-weighted values weight each cell by the number of people estimated to live there. Each headline number was recomputed with a second, independent method, and both results matched. Every number in this story is listed, with the file and script that produced it, in analysis/FINDINGS.md.

    What the data cannot tell us

    • The air temperature people feel at street level. The satellite measures surfaces.
    • Night-time heat. Landsat passes in the morning; it cannot show how slowly places cool at night.
    • Afternoon peaks, which are usually hotter than the morning overpass.
    • Heat inside homes, including under iron sheet roofs.
    • Detail finer than about 100 m.
    • Why a place is hot. The links with trees and buildings are associations, not measured causes.
    • Who is most at risk. That also depends on age, health, work, housing and access to water and cooling.
    • Exact populations. WorldPop numbers are modelled and may undercount the densest settlements.
    • Whether the city's ground is warming over time. There are too few clear scenes each year to tell.

Mapping urban heat and cooling inequality across Nairobi

Land surface temperatures derived from clear-sky Landsat 8 and 9 satellite imagery on January to March mornings (2023 to 2026), overlaid with the spatial distributions of residents, children, schools, and tree canopy across Nairobi's warmer and cooler ground.

On clear January to March mornings, half of Nairobi County's land area recorded surface temperatures above {{q1.county_median_c|c}}, with ground in and around the Industrial Area registering {{x.copy_v3.industrial_and_around.gap_c|c}} warmer than Karura Forest and the Nairobi Arboretum. In this analysis, the area exceeding that median threshold is defined as the city's "hotter half", and it is where the majority of Nairobi resides: approximately {{x.people_by_heat.hotter_half.people|int}} people ({{x.people_by_heat.hotter_half.share_pct|pct}} of county residents), including roughly {{x.children_by_heat.sides.hotter half.school_age_4_17|int}} school-aged children between 4 and 17 years old.

Two kinds of picture from space

The true-colour ground satellite imagery in this analysis comes from the European Space Agency's Sentinel-2 satellites, compiled into a cloud-free mosaic by EOX, with place and street names from Mapbox and OpenStreetMap, showing physical land cover including roofs, roads, trees, and bare soil. The thermal data comes from a separate instrument: thermal sensors aboard NASA and the US Geological Survey's Landsat 8 and 9 satellites, which measure surface temperatures as they pass over Nairobi at approximately {{q0.overpass_local|raw}} am. The maps display the median land surface temperature across {{q0.n_hot_scenes|raw}} clear mornings from January to March, 2023 to 2026.

That reading is a land surface temperature, which is the heat of roofs, roads, soil, and treetops. On a sunny morning, it can be far hotter than the air a person breathes one to two metres above the ground. Because hot surfaces warm the air around them, ground surface temperature and air temperature are closely linked; nevertheless, every satellite temperature in this analysis measures the temperature of the ground, not of the air.

Nairobi's heat falls unevenly

Nairobi is often described as a city with a mild climate, and on most days that still holds. Yet on clear mornings in the hot season, the ground under some neighbourhoods gets far hotter than the ground under others. The roofs, roads, and bare yards of the east and the Industrial Area heat up fast, while Karura Forest, Ngong Road Forest, and the leafy suburbs in the west stay much cooler.

Most of Nairobi's people live on its hotter ground

Imagine dividing all of Nairobi County into tiny 30-metre squares and lining them up from coolest to hottest based on clear, hot-season mornings. If you cut that line right down the middle at {{q1.county_median_c|c}}, both halves cover the exact same amount of land, yet the number of people living on each side is completely uneven. About {{x.people_by_heat.hotter_half.people|int}} people ({{x.people_by_heat.hotter_half.share_pct|pct}} of the county) live on the hotter half, compared with about {{x.people_by_heat.cooler_half.people|int}} on the cooler half. That means for every person living on cool ground, more than two people live on hot ground.

Now take that same line of squares and divide it into five equal-sized parts, stepping up from coolest to hottest. The coolest fifth, which covers the city's forests and much of Karen and Kitisuru, is home to only about {{x.people_by_heat.fifths.0.people|int}} people ({{x.people_by_heat.fifths.0.share_pct|pct}} of the county). Jump up to the fourth fifth, where the ground reaches {{x.people_by_heat.thresholds_c.p60|c}} to {{x.people_by_heat.thresholds_c.p80|c}}, and the population shoots up to about {{x.people_by_heat.fifths.3.people|int}} people ({{x.people_by_heat.fifths.3.share_pct|pct}} of the county), nearly eight times as many people living on the exact same amount of land. The very hottest fifth holds about {{x.people_by_heat.fifths.4.people|int}} people. That is still three times as many people as the coolest fifth, and it is only lower than the fourth fifth because much of that hottest land is bare, open ground around Ruai and the airport where few people live.

The hottest wards have the fewest trees

Areas with more trees were cooler: hot-season surface temperature fell from {{q3.tree_band_0_10_mean_c|c}} where tree cover was under 10% to {{q3.tree_band_90_100_mean_c|c}} where it was over 90%.

The ten hottest wards where people live averaged {{x.tables_v2.ward_cover_extremes.hottest_mean_tree_pct|pct}} tree cover, compared with {{x.tables_v2.ward_cover_extremes.coolest_mean_tree_pct|pct}} in the ten coolest.

The hotter half of Nairobi is home to most of its children, just like most adults

Approximately {{x.children_by_heat.sides.hotter half.school_age_4_17|int}} school-aged children (ages 4 to 17) live on the hotter half of Nairobi's land area. That represents {{x.children_by_heat.sides.hotter half.school_age_4_17_share_pct|pct}} of the county's estimated {{x.children_by_heat.sides.county.school_age_4_17|int}} children, closely matching the {{x.children_by_heat.context.adults_hotter_half_share_pct|pct}} share of adults residing on hotter ground. What sets children apart is both their higher physiological vulnerability to heat stress and the fact that they spend their days inside classrooms during the hottest hours.

Schools on the hot side have almost no trees around them

The difference between Nairobi's hotter and cooler ground is clearest in the land around its schools. Within 200 m of the {{x.schools_trees_200m.fifths.0.n_schools|int}} schools on the coolest fifth of the county's land, a median of {{x.schools_trees_200m.fifths.0.median_tree_pct|pct}} of the ground is under trees. Around the {{x.schools_trees_200m.fifths.4.n_schools|int}} schools on the hottest fifth, that median drops to {{x.schools_trees_200m.fifths.4.median_tree_pct|pct}}, and at {{x.schools_trees_200m.fifths.4.share_under_10pct_trees|pct}} of those schools, less than a tenth of the land within 200 m is under trees.

Nairobi's ground is hottest during class hours

Landsat captures Nairobi only during its morning overpass, at about {{q0.overpass_local|raw}} am. The chart above tracks a full day using a different dataset: the Copernicus hourly land surface temperature record for 27 January 2026, a Tuesday in the fourth week of the first school term. The county's ground warmed quickly once the sun was up, reached {{x.copy_v3.day_of_heat.county_peak_c|c}} at {{x.copy_v3.day_of_heat.county_peak_hour|raw}}, and stayed close to that level until 14:00.

Kenya's day schools hold classes from 08:00 to 15:30 on weekdays under Regulation 84 of the Basic Education Regulations, 2015, which puts the hottest hours of the day directly inside school hours. The first term of 2026 also ran from 5 January to 2 April, the same hot, dry months that these maps describe.

What it means

Children heat up faster than adults and struggle to learn in hot classrooms. In Nairobi, most children live, and most of their schools stand, on the hotter half of the city, where the ground around schools has the least shade. There are three interventions worth testing first: planting shade trees in school yards and along the roads children walk to the hottest schools, installing insulated ceilings under iron sheet roofs, and placing sensors inside classrooms to track the air temperatures satellites cannot see.

Each of these steps sits with different institutions. Under Kenya's Constitution, the national government oversees primary and secondary schools through the Ministry of Education, while Nairobi City County handles pre-primary education, county planning, and county parks. Setting shade and cooling targets for school grounds will take both levels of government and a line in the county's annual budget. The city's gazetted forests, including Karura Forest, are managed by the Kenya Forest Service, a national agency, alongside community groups such as the Friends of Karura Forest. Schools, parents, and community organisations such as iRise are best placed to lead the greening of school yards and roofs, giving donors a direct way to support work communities already want, while universities and research groups are best placed to run the classroom sensor studies.

      The same places, morning after morning

      Each frame of this animation is one clear hot-season morning between 2014 and 2026. Some mornings were hotter than others, so each frame shows how much hotter or cooler each place was than the county median on that same morning: red ground was hotter than the rest of the county, and blue ground cooler.

      As the mornings go by, the colours stay in much the same places. The east, from Kasarani and Dandora out to Ruai, and the land around the Industrial Area are red on most mornings, while Karura Forest, Karen and the forests in the west are blue; the heat divide is part of the city's ground, and it comes back every hot season.

      The same neighbourhoods are the hottest in both dry seasons

      Nairobi has two dry seasons: a hot one from January to March and a cool one from June to August. In the cool season the whole city's ground is cooler (county median {{q1.cool_season_county_median_c|c}}, against {{q1.county_median_c|c}} in the hot season). But look at where the red is in the two maps below: the centre, the east and Ruai are hot in both, and Karura, Karen and the forests are cool in both. Ranked by surface temperature, the wards fall in a similar order in both seasons (rank correlation {{q1.hot_cool_ward_spearman|raw}}, where 1 would be the same order and 0 no link).

      Most new building went up on the city's edges

      Between 1990 and 2020, built-up surface in Nairobi County grew from about {{q6.built_km2.1990|km2}} to about {{q6.built_km2.2020|km2}}, around {{q6.built_ratio_2020_1990|d1}} times as much.

      Between 1990 and 2020, {{x.copy_v3.growth.top4_added_share_pct|pct}} of the new built-up land was in four outer sub-counties: Kasarani in the north-east, Embakasi East in the east, Langata in the south-west and Westlands in the north-west. The older, dense areas near the centre, such as Mathare, Kamukunji and Starehe, changed little, because they were already about as built up in 1990 as they are today.
